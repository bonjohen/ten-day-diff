This is The Ten-Day Diff for Wednesday, September 30th.

I'm covering what changed since Monday's episode, and there's a fair amount, especially on the Claude side.

Let's start with AI and software.

Technical - AI/software

xAI shipped Grok 4.7 on September 21.

It's a bigger model, about 2.1 trillion parameters, up from 1.5 trillion in Grok 4.6.

Pricing is $2 per million input tokens and $6 per million output. xAI says it spends longer on hard problems and checks its own answers more often.

On the published leaderboards it lands second, behind the current frontier leader.

It's already in the xAI API, Cursor and GitHub Copilot.

Why it matters to you.

At $6 output, it's a cheap second opinion for long reasoning jobs. If your pipeline has a model routing layer, it's worth a bake-off on a few of your own tasks before you believe any leaderboard.

The second item came out of a Claude blog post on September 28.

NVIDIA released OpenShell, an open-source runtime that sits under an agent and enforces policy on every action it takes. File access, network calls, and data movement are all denied by default unless a rule allows them.

It's Apache 2.0 licensed and on GitHub.

Anthropic paired it with Claude Managed Agents, which keep credentials in a vault the agent never sees and write an audit trail of what the agent did.

For you, this is the missing piece if you want agents touching HubSpot or Snowflake without handing them raw keys.

Default deny, plus a log you can show a security reviewer, is a much easier conversation.

Technical - Materials science

Materials science.

In Nature on September 23rd, a team from National Taiwan Normal University, MIT, and National Taiwan University grew bilayer molybdenum disulfide by chemical vapor deposition with a specific 1H stacking. Normally, stacking a second layer turns molybdenum disulfide's gap from direct to indirect, and the light emission drops off.

With this stacking, the bilayer keeps a direct band gap.

Emission gets stronger, and valley polarization is actually better than in a single layer.

That's a clean example of stacking order as a design knob, not just layer count.

If you've ever modeled 2D materials, this is a structure-property result you can reproduce in DFT.

Same issue of Nature, same day. A group at the Max Planck Institute for Polymer Research designed nine-residue peptides that assemble into hexagonal, honeycomb-like fibrils.

The sequence sets the lattice, and each fibril carries continuous channels about 5 nanometers wide.

They can build single-channel or multi-channel versions.

It's sequence-programmed porosity, which is a nice bridge between self-assembly and the kind of design-space search you'd normally do with code.

And on September 24th, TU Dortmund reported in Nature Communications that separate time crystals in the same gallium arsenide chip, doped with indium and silicon, and cooled to about -270 degrees Celsius, locked to a common frequency.

They sync across as much as 40 micrometers, coupled through spin-polarized electrons.

That's over a thousand times the size of one oscillator.

It's early physics, but long-range coupling between oscillators in a solid is the sort of thing that later shows up in sensing and timing hardware.

DIY electronics

DIY electronics.

On September 29, CNX Software covered the Walnut Pi CM2, a compute module built on the Allwinner T527.

It has 8 Cortex-A55 cores, up to 4 GB of RAM, a 2 TOPS NPU, Wi-Fi 6, and PCI Express. Pricing runs from about $28 to $49 on AliExpress.

The comparable Raspberry Pi CM5 is closer to $98. It isn't pin-compatible with the CM5, so plan on its own carrier board.

Same day, NXP's FRDM-IMXRT1186 board.

It's a crossover MCU with a Cortex-M7 at 800 MHz and a Cortex-M33 at 300 MHz. What stands out is four Ethernet ports: 2 gigabit for time-sensitive networking and 2 Fast Ethernet that can run EtherCAT.

It's $85 and supports Zephyr and FreeRTOS.

That's an unusually cheap way to learn industrial Ethernet.

And on September 28th, Critical Link announced the MitySOM-AM62 family: three pin-compatible SO-DIMM modules on TI Sitara AM62 chips. One is general purpose, one adds a 2 TOPS DSP for vision, and one has a stronger GPU for displays.

Dev kits run about $500 to $670.

It's more industrial than hobby, but it lets you prototype on one carrier and swap the compute later.

Data regulation

Data regulation.

On September 25th, the Senate Judiciary Subcommittee on Crime held a hearing on Flock Safety's camera network, chaired by Senator Josh Hawley, with Senators Durbin and Padilla taking part.

The numbers came up again and again: about 120,000 cameras in 49 states and roughly 20 billion vehicle images a month.

Flock's CEO declined to testify.

Warrant requirements for access to that data were the main reform on the table.

This matters because it's bipartisan.

When license plate data gets federal attention, the rules for any data broker feeding location or vehicle signals into a sales or enrichment pipeline tend to follow.

Second, the Australian government opened an investigation on September 24th after OpenAI models accessed government websites during internal training and evaluation back in June.

The sites included Services Australia's Medicare statistics portal. OpenAI apologized publicly on September 29.

It says it found no access to individual medical or criminal records, and it's setting up an independent task force with Australian experts.

The takeaway for you is simple. An agent that browses on your behalf can create legal exposure for you even when nobody meant it to.

Scoping which domains an agent may touch isn't optional anymore.

One quick note.

SB 690, the California pen register reform we covered Monday, is still on the governor's desk.

Today is his deadline and I haven't seen a signature reported yet.

Claude for GTM engineering

Last, Claude for GTM engineering.

The big one is Claude Sonnet 5.5, released September 28.

It costs $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output.

Anthropic says it's more than 30% faster than Sonnet 5 and scores within a point or two of Opus 5.5 on several agent benchmarks. The model ID is claude-sonnet-5-5.

Watch for breaking changes if you're migrating from Sonnet 5.

Forced tool use with tool_choice set to any or tool now returns a 400 error, and the disabled thinking setting is replaced.

For your pipeline, this is probably the new default for high-volume jobs like call summarization from Gong and enrichment writes into HubSpot.

Check your tool_choice settings before you flip the model name.

On September 24, two platform changes. Anthropic resumed billing for some refusals that happen before any output in three narrow categories.

Separately, the Compliance API activity feed stopped returning file names and titles, including on past records.

If you have any audit or reporting job reading that feed, the title fields are now empty.

Don't let a dashboard quietly go blank.

And Claude Code shipped two releases worth knowing.

Version 2.1.283 on September 25th added a /doctor prompt-audit command that reviews your CLAUDE.md files, and it now sends MCP tool outputs to OpenTelemetry.

Version 2.1.284 on September 28th added a /mcp reconnect all command and fixed tool calls failing in resumed sessions.

The OpenTelemetry change is the useful one for you.

You can finally see what your Snowflake or HubSpot MCP tools actually returned when a run goes sideways.
